Abstract

Modern enterprise and institutional cybersecurity perimeters face an unprecedented crisis driven by the weaponization of Artificial Intelligence. Threat actors deploy Large Language Models (LLMs) to synthesize grammatically flawless, contextually authentic, and polymorphic phishing lures that evade traditional signature-based Antivirus (AV) gateways and static regular expression filters. Crucially, standard email filtering controls suffer from two foundational deficiencies: (1) Binary Oversimplification, collapsing benign, commercial bulk spam, and deceptive fraud into coarse categories, and (2) The Black-Box Dilemma, where opaque machine learning classifiers output threat classifications without interpretable justification, leaving Security Operations Center (SOC) analysts incapable of validating quarantine decisions.

To resolve these operational and theoretical challenges, this project presents MailForenSIX, an end-to-end artificial intelligence platform engineered for multi-class email threat detection, model interpretability, and cryptographic protocol validation in accordance with the 23AI2001 (Artificial Intelligence for Cyber Security) curriculum.

The core contributions of this work are fourfold:

  1. Curated Balanced 3-Class Benchmark Corpus: An empirical dataset of 31,164 emails uniformly balanced across Class 0: Legitimate (10,388), Class 1: Spam (10,388), and Class 2: Phishing/BEC (10,388), consolidated across 8 standard benchmark corpora.
  2. Multi-Model Algorithmic Benchmark Suite: Systematic training, tuning, and evaluation of all five machine learning models explicitly specified in Syllabus Module 2: Multinomial Naive Bayes (Generative probabilistic), Linear Support Vector Machines (Max-margin), Logistic Regression (L2 regularized log-odds), Decision Trees (CART), and Gradient Boosted Trees. Linear SVM achieved the peak overall performance with 93.36% Accuracy and a Macro F1-Score of 0.9336 at an ultra-low inference latency of 2.1 microseconds per sample.
  3. Explainable AI (LIME) Multi-Class Attribution: Overcoming black-box opacity via Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (Pan & Mishra, 2023), computing token-level and sentence-level weight attribution heatmaps that expose the specific lexical indicators driving class assignment.
  4. Account & Domain Reputation Scoring Engine (ADRSE): Operationalizing Module 5 by synthesizing SPF alignment, DKIM cryptographic cryptanalysis, and DMARC enforcement policies into an automated 0–100 asset protection index.

Chapter 1: Introduction to AI for Cyber Security

1.1 Evolution of Email Threats: From Spam to Polymorphic Phishing

Since the standardization of the Simple Mail Transfer Protocol (SMTP, RFC 821/5321), electronic mail has remained the lifeblood of global commerce, governance, and organizational workflows. Consequently, it represents the single most frequently exploited attack vector across all cybersecurity incident taxonomies.

Historically, email threats were characterized by high-volume, opportunistic spam—unsolicited commercial advertisements broadcast indiscriminately to millions of recipients. Early defenses successfully mitigated spam using static IP blacklists (DNSBL), regular expression pattern matching, and primitive Bayesian keyword filters.

Over the past five years, however, adversary tactics have experienced a seismic paradigm shift. Cybercriminal syndicates and Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) now specialize in targeted phishing, Executive Impersonation, and Business Email Compromise (BEC). Attackers employ Generative Artificial Intelligence (LLMs) to automatically synthesize personalized, grammatically perfect communication lures that mimic trusted institutional styles (e.g., banks, tax authorities, corporate executives). This AI-driven polymorphism renders classical lexical rules completely obsolete.

1.2 The Problem: Signature Antivirus Limits & Black-Box Inscrutability

To understand the imperative for AI in modern email defense, one must examine the critical limitations of legacy perimeter tools (directly addressing Course Outcome 2: Predict the antivirus limits in threat detection):

The Fundamental Antivirus Blind Spot (Signature Inadequacy)
Traditional Antivirus (AV) gateways rely upon cryptographic hashing (MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256) and static byte sequence scanning. When an attacker modifies a single word, alters a URL parameter, or introduces zero-width whitespace characters into an email body, the resulting SHA-256 checksum changes completely:
SHA256(Lure_A) != SHA256(Lure_A_with_whitespace)
Consequently, signature-based AV gateways exhibit a 0% detection rate against zero-hour, generative-AI phishing variants.

Furthermore, standard email defenses evaluate messages using opaque "threat scores" without explainability. In an enterprise SOC or judicial evidentiary setting, an analyst cannot justify quarantining an executive communication or initiating financial clawback procedures based merely on a black-box output of "Phishing (92%)". Without interpretable local feature attributions (as mandated by Explainable AI for Cybersecurity, Pan & Mishra, 2023), automated security creates operational friction and analytical paralysis.

2.2 Mathematical Formulations of the 5 Syllabus Classifiers

1. Multinomial Naive Bayes (Spam Detection with Naive Bayes)

Multinomial Naive Bayes is a generative probabilistic classifier rooted in Bayes' Theorem. Given an email represented as a vector of term frequencies x = (x_1, x_2, ..., x_V) over vocabulary V, the posterior probability of class c in {0, 1, 2} is given by:

P(c | x) = [ P(c) · ∏_{i=1}^V P(w_i | c)^{x_i} ] / P(x)

Under the Naive Bayes assumption of conditional feature independence, and applying log-space transformation to prevent floating-point underflow, the decision rule is formulated as:

c* = argmax_{c} [ log P(c) + ∑_{i=1}^V x_i · log P(w_i | c) ]

To prevent zero-probability collapse for out-of-vocabulary terms during inference, Laplace (Additive) Smoothing (alpha = 1.0) is applied to term likelihoods:

P(w_i | c) = ( N_{ci} + α ) / ( N_c + α·|V| )

where N_{ci} is the count of term w_i in class c, N_c is total term count in class c, and |V| = 10,000.

2. Linear Support Vector Machine (Spam Detection with SVMs)

Support Vector Machines construct a maximum-margin separating hyperplane in high-dimensional TF-IDF space. For multi-class separation, a One-vs-Rest (OvR) formulation is employed. The primal soft-margin optimization problem with slack variables ξ_i is formulated as:

min_{w, b, ξ} (1/2) ||w||² + C · ∑_{i=1}^N ξ_i subject to: y_i ( wᵀ x_i + b ) ≥ 1 - ξ_i, ξ_i ≥ 0, ∀i

The objective penalizes training instances falling inside the margin via the Hinge Loss function:

L_hinge(y, f(x)) = max(0, 1 - y · f(x))

Parameter C = 1.0 controls the trade-off between maximizing the geometric margin 2/||w|| and minimizing classification errors.

3. Logistic Regression (Phishing Detection with Logistic Regression)

Logistic Regression models class posterior probabilities directly using the Softmax function (Multinomial Logistic Regression). The predicted probability for class k in {0, 1, 2} given feature vector x_i is:

P(y_i = k | x_i) = exp( w_kᵀ x_i + b_k ) / ∑_{j=0}^{K-1} exp( w_jᵀ x_i + b_j )

Model parameters are estimated by minimizing the Categorical Cross-Entropy (Log-Loss) objective augmented with an L2 weight regularization term to prevent overfitting on sparse n-gram features:

J(W) = - (1/N) ∑_{i=1}^N ∑_{k=0}^2 I(y_i = k) · log P(y_i = k | x_i) + (λ/2) ∑_{k=0}^2 ||w_k||²

4. Decision Tree (Phishing Detection with Decision Trees)

The Decision Tree classifier recursively partitions the feature space into orthogonal rectangular hyperplanes using the CART (Classification and Regression Trees) algorithm. At each internal node t, the algorithm selects the optimal split on feature j at threshold θ that maximizes the reduction in Gini Impurity:

Gini(t) = 1 - ∑_{k=0}^2 p(k | t)² ΔGini(s, t) = Gini(t) - [ (N_L / N_t) · Gini(t_L) + (N_R / N_t) · Gini(t_R) ]

To prevent tree memorization and overfitting on sparse text matrices, tree depth is constrained to max_depth = 35 with min_samples_split = 5.

5. Gradient Boosted Trees (Ensemble Tree Threat Detectors)

Gradient Boosting trains an ensemble of M shallow regression trees in an additive sequential manner: F_M(x) = ∑_{m=1}^M γ_m h_m(x). At each boosting step m, a new tree h_m is fit to the pseudo-residuals (negative gradient of the loss function):

r_{ikm} = - [ ∂ L(y_i, F(x_i)) / ∂ F_k(x_i) ]_{F(x) = F_{m-1}(x)}

The implementation utilizes HistGradientBoostingClassifier, which discretizes continuous TF-IDF feature values into 256 integer bins, drastically accelerating split finding from O(N log N) to O(N).

3.3 NLP Feature Tokenization & Cyber Domain Tokens

Standard NLP tokenizers discard non-alphabetic structures, ignoring vital threat indicators. MailForenSIX implements a custom regular expression substitution engine that injects specialized synthetic tokens into the lexical stream prior to vectorization:

  • URL_TOKEN: Replaces HTTP/HTTPS hyperlinks, neutralizing URL obfuscation while preserving the structural signal that links are present.
  • IP_TOKEN: Replaces raw IPv4 addresses, identifying unauthenticated direct-to-IP routing patterns.
  • CURRENCY_TOKEN: Normalizes monetary references ($, USD, €, wire transfer), flagging advance-fee fraud and invoice modification.
  • URGENCY_TOKEN: Matches urgency cues ("account suspended", "verify immediately", "action required").

The vectorizer applies Sublinear Term Frequency scaling: tf_sublinear = 1 + log(tf) for tf > 0, preventing repetitive keyword-stuffing attacks from overwhelming the feature space.

Chapter 4: Explainable AI (LIME) & Protocol Authentication

4.1 The Black-Box Dilemma & LIME Mathematical Framework

In digital forensics and regulatory compliance (DPDP Act 2023, IT Act 2000 Section 65B), machine learning predictions cannot serve as admissible evidence without an auditable chain of reasoning. Following the foundational curriculum reference (Explainable AI for Cybersecurity, Pan & Mishra, 2023), MailForenSIX integrates Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME).

Let f: R^d -> R^K represent the complex, black-box classification model. LIME explains the prediction for a specific email x by approximating f locally with an interpretable surrogate model g in G (a sparse linear regressor). The objective is formulated as:

ξ(x) = argmin_{g ∈ G} L(f, g, π_x) + Ω(g)

where:

  • L(f, g, π_x) = ∑_{z, z' ∈ Z} π_x(z) [ f(z) - g(z') ]² is the squared loss of g approximating f over perturbed instances z' sampled in the neighborhood of x.
  • π_x(z) = exp( - D(x, z)² / σ² ) is an exponential kernel defining the proximity measure between the original email x and perturbed instance z using cosine distance.
  • Ω(g) = ∞ · I( |w| > K ) enforces model interpretability by constraining the number of non-zero feature attribution weights to top K = 10.
